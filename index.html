Why is human oversight necessary?
Criminal Justice · COMPAS Recidivism Algorithm
US courts used a machine-learning algorithm to score defendants' risk of reoffending, trusting the number as neutral. A 2016 investigation found it was nearly twice as likely to wrongly flag Black defendants as high-risk, and nobody had checked the model for that bias before judges started relying on it. The investigation's own headline called it ‘biased against blacks.’
— ProPublica, “Machine Bias,” 2016
Welfare & Tax · Dutch Childcare Benefits Scandal
A self-learning algorithm decided which parents looked like fraud risks, using dual nationality as one of its signals. Over 20,000 families were wrongly accused, forced into debt, and in thousands of cases had their children taken into foster care, before anyone with real authority checked what the algorithm was actually doing. The government resigned after a parliamentary inquiry found ‘grave shortcomings, including institutional biases.’
— Dutch parliamentary inquiry report, 2020
Hiring · Amazon's AI Recruiting Tool
Amazon built a machine-learning tool to score job applicants automatically, training it on ten years of past resumes. Nobody caught, for a full year, that it had taught itself to penalize any resume mentioning ‘women's’ — Reuters later put it simply: the tool ‘did not like women.’
— Reuters, Jeffrey Dastin, 2018
Education · UK A-level Grading Algorithm, 2020
In August 2020, with exams cancelled by the pandemic, UK exam regulator Ofqual used an algorithm to calculate students' final grades instead of trusting teachers' own predictions. Nearly 40% of A-level results were downgraded, hitting state school students hardest while boosting private school results, with no real way to challenge a grade before it cost someone their university place. The education secretary said he was ‘incredibly sorry’ as the government was forced into a U-turn days later.
— CNBC / Ofqual results data, 2020

WHY THIS EXISTS

The gap between regulatory language and practical implementation

Most oversight definitions sound plausible in a document. They name values, invoke principles, and gesture toward accountability. But when an AI system behaves unexpectedly, definitions are stress-tested in ways their authors never imagined.

Signal Check asks four concrete questions of any definition: Who is the human in the loop? When do they look? How would they know something is wrong? And can they actually do anything about it?
