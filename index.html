Why is human oversight necessary?
Criminal Justice · COMPAS Recidivism Algorithm
A machine-learning algorithm scored defendants' risk of reoffending — nearly twice as likely to wrongly flag Black defendants as high-risk. Nobody had checked the model for that bias before judges started relying on it.
— ProPublica, “Machine Bias,” 2016
Welfare & Tax · Dutch Childcare Benefits Scandal
A self-learning algorithm flagged parents as fraud risks, using dual nationality as a signal. Over 20,000 families were wrongly accused, plunged into debt, and in thousands of cases had their children taken into foster care.
— Dutch parliamentary inquiry report, 2020
Hiring · Amazon's AI Recruiting Tool
Amazon's hiring algorithm taught itself to penalize any resume mentioning ‘women's’ — and nobody caught it for a full year. Reuters put it simply: the tool ‘did not like women.’
— Reuters, Jeffrey Dastin, 2018
Education · UK A-level Grading Algorithm, 2020
With exams cancelled in 2020, Ofqual used an algorithm to calculate final grades instead of trusting teachers. Nearly 40% of results were downgraded — hitting state school students hardest, with no real way to challenge a grade before it cost someone their place.
— CNBC / Ofqual results data, 2020

WHY THIS EXISTS

The gap between regulatory language and practical implementation

Most oversight definitions sound plausible in a document. They name values, invoke principles, and gesture toward accountability. But when an AI system behaves unexpectedly, definitions are stress-tested in ways their authors never imagined.

Signal Check asks four concrete questions of any definition: Who is the human in the loop? When do they look? How would they know something is wrong? And can they actually do anything about it?
